Finance and Economics (Paper-III) · Tier-II Paper-III, AAO and Assistant Accounts Officer only

Indian economy

The Indian-economy part of Paper-III overlaps the General Awareness page, and then asks for a fuller account: sectors, national income methods, poverty, unemployment, infrastructure and the 1991 reforms. Use the definitions exactly. A current percentage belongs in your affairs file, not in a frozen note.

National income can be measured from output, from incomes, or from expenditure. In principle the three meet. In practice the statistical office reconciles them.

What SSC lists

  • Role of agriculture, industry and services.
  • National income concepts and the three methods.
  • Population, poverty, unemployment and infrastructure.
  • Reforms since 1991: liberalisation, privatisation, globalisation and disinvestment.

Rules and formulas

Sectors

Agriculture still employs a large share of the workforce relative to its share of output. That gap is part of the disguised-unemployment story.
Industry includes manufacturing. Services include trade, transport, finance and public administration, and they are the largest share of output in the current Indian economy. The shares move, so quote a fresh Economic Survey if a question wants a number.

National income

GDP: final goods and services produced inside the domestic territory.
GNP = GDP + net factor income from abroad.
NNP = GNP − depreciation.
The three methods are the product (value-added) method, the income method and the expenditure method.

Poverty and work

Absolute poverty is measured against a fixed minimum. Relative poverty compares people with each other.
Unemployment types: frictional (between jobs), structural (skills do not match jobs), cyclical (a downturn), and disguised (extra workers with almost no extra output).

1991

The reform package is liberalisation, privatisation and globalisation.
Disinvestment is the sale of the government’s stake in a public enterprise.
Infrastructure in the syllabus means energy, transport and communication, the base that other production uses.

Common traps

GNP is not “GDP minus depreciation”. Depreciation takes you from gross to net. Net factor income takes you from domestic to national.

A larger services share of GDP does not mean most workers are in services. Check which share the question means.

Disguised unemployment is not the same as a person openly looking for work.

Do not memorise a poverty ratio from an old book. The line and the ratio are revised.

Worked examples

Read the solution only after you have tried the question. Options are in exam style.

Example 1
GNP equals

  1. GDP minus depreciation
  2. GDP plus net factor income from abroad
  3. NNP plus indirect taxes only
  4. exports minus imports

Solution

  1. Domestic becomes national by adding net factor income from abroad.
  2. Depreciation is the step from gross to net.

Answer: B. NNP at market prices would be GNP minus depreciation.

Example 2
The three methods of measuring national income are

  1. mean, median and mode
  2. product, income and expenditure
  3. Laspeyres, Paasche and Fisher
  4. primary, secondary and tertiary as methods of counting

Solution

  1. Output, incomes and spending are the three routes.
  2. The sector names are a classification, not the three methods.

Answer: B. Index-number formulas belong to statistics.

Example 3
Disinvestment means

  1. a cut in the repo rate
  2. sale of the government’s stake in a public enterprise
  3. a new poverty line
  4. printing currency

Solution

  1. The government sells equity it holds.
  2. That is disinvestment.

Answer: B. It is part of the privatisation story, not a monetary-policy rate.

Example 4
Absolute poverty is

  1. a comparison with a neighbour’s income only
  2. a shortfall against a fixed minimum
  3. the same as frictional unemployment
  4. a stock-market fall

Solution

  1. The minimum is a line.
  2. Falling below it is absolute poverty.
  3. Relative poverty is the comparison with other people.

Answer: B. Both ideas are in the syllabus. The question names absolute.
