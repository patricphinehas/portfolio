Finance and Economics (Paper-III) · Tier-II Paper-III, AAO and Assistant Accounts Officer only

Accounting principles

Paper-III is only for candidates shortlisted for Assistant Audit Officer or Assistant Accounts Officer. Part A is Finance and Accounts, for 80 of the 200 marks. It starts from the idea that every transaction has two sides, and from the concepts that stop those two sides being recorded in an arbitrary way.

The accounting equation is the check on every later page: assets equal liabilities plus capital.

What SSC lists

  • Nature, scope and limitations of financial accounting.
  • Basic concepts and conventions, and generally accepted accounting principles.
  • Single entry and double entry.
  • Capital and revenue expenditure, as a classification you will use again in the final accounts.

Rules and formulas

Equation

Assets = liabilities + capital.
If a transaction increases an asset and increases capital, the equation still balances. If it only increases an asset, something else must have changed.

Concepts

Business entity: the owner and the business are separate.
Going concern: the business is assumed to continue.
Money measurement: only what can be measured in money is recorded.
Cost: assets are recorded at cost, not at a guessed resale value, unless a particular standard says otherwise.
Accrual: record income and expense when they are earned or incurred, not only when cash moves.

Double entry

Every transaction has a debit and a credit of equal amount.
Personal accounts: debit the receiver, credit the giver.
Real accounts: debit what comes in, credit what goes out.
Nominal accounts: debit expenses and losses, credit incomes and gains.
Single entry does not record both sides fully. It is incomplete, and a statement of affairs is used to estimate the result.

Capital and revenue

Capital expenditure creates a benefit that lasts beyond the current year, or acquires a fixed asset. It goes to the balance sheet.
Revenue expenditure is the ordinary running cost of the year. It goes to the profit and loss account.
A repair that merely keeps an asset working is revenue. A new machine is capital.

Common traps

A debit does not mean “bad” and a credit does not mean “good”. They are sides of the account.

The owner’s drawings reduce capital. They are not a business expense of the profit and loss account.

Recording only cash is not double entry.

A capital expense wrongly put in the profit and loss account understates the asset and understates profit.

Worked examples

Read the solution only after you have tried the question. Options are in exam style.

Example 1
The accounting equation is

  1. Assets = liabilities − capital
  2. Assets = liabilities + capital
  3. Capital = assets + liabilities
  4. Income = assets

Solution

  1. What the business controls is funded by outsiders or by the owner.
  2. Assets = liabilities + capital.

Answer: B. Rearranged, capital = assets − liabilities. That is not the same as capital = assets + liabilities.

Example 2
Debit the receiver and credit the giver is the rule for

  1. personal accounts
  2. real accounts
  3. nominal accounts
  4. only the cash account in every system

Solution

  1. A person who receives value is debited.
  2. The person who gives value is credited.
  3. That is the personal-account rule.

Answer: A. Real accounts use “debit what comes in”. Nominal accounts use “debit expenses”.

Example 3
Purchase of a machine for long-term use is

  1. revenue expenditure
  2. capital expenditure
  3. a drawing
  4. a loss by definition

Solution

  1. The benefit lasts beyond the year.
  2. The machine is an asset.
  3. The spending is capital expenditure.

Answer: B. Ordinary repairs of that machine, later, are revenue.

Example 4
The accrual concept requires that

  1. cash is the only event
  2. income is recorded when earned, not only when cash arrives
  3. assets are revalued every day
  4. drawings are an expense

Solution

  1. Earning and receipt can fall in different periods.
  2. Accrual records the earning.

Answer: B. A purely cash record would miss income that has been earned but not yet received.
