Quantitative Aptitude · Tier-I and Paper-I

Tables, graphs and basic statistics

This is the statistics that Paper-I actually asks: read a table or a chart, then find a total, an average, or a percent. Mean, median, mode and a simple standard deviation also sit in the same line of the syllabus.

The heavier statistics paper, for JSO posts only, is a separate set of pages. Do not mix the two levels while you are learning Paper-I.

What SSC lists

  • Histogram, frequency polygon, bar diagram and pie chart.
  • Reading a table and comparing entries.
  • Mean, median and mode.
  • Standard deviation of a short ungrouped list, and a simple use of the empirical relation.

Rules and formulas

Charts

A bar chart compares separate categories and has gaps.
A histogram is for continuous classes. The area of a bar represents frequency.
A pie chart is a full circle of 360°. A sector’s value = (angle/360) × total.
A frequency polygon joins the midpoints of the tops of histogram bars.

Averages of a list

Mean = sum / count.
Median: sort the list. If the count is odd, take the middle. If even, take the mean of the two middle values.
Mode is the most frequent value. Two equally frequent values make the list bimodal.

Spread

For a list of n values, the school-level standard deviation used here is √[ (sum of squared deviations from the mean) / n ].
Variance is the square of the standard deviation.

Empirical relation

For a moderately skewed distribution, mode ≈ 3 × median − 2 × mean.
This is an approximation, not an identity.

Quick methods

Pie chart: percent = angle/360 × 100, and angle = percent × 3.6.
Growth from year 1 to year 2 = change / year-1 value × 100.
Cancel common zeros before dividing big table numbers.
Median of an even count = mean of the two middle values after sorting.

Common traps

A pie-chart angle is not itself the answer unless the question asks for degrees. Convert it with the total.

Median needs the list sorted first.

The empirical mode formula can disagree with the actual mode of a small list. Use it only when the question invokes it.

Dividing the squared deviations by n, not by n−1, is the form to use for this matric-level line of the syllabus.

Worked examples

Read the solution only after you have tried the question. Options are in exam style.

Example 1
The productions in four years are 40, 50, 30 and 80 units. The average production is

  1. 45
  2. 50
  3. 55
  4. 60

Solution

  1. Sum = 40 + 50 + 30 + 80 = 200.
  2. Average = 200/4 = 50.

Answer: B. The jump to 80 does not change the method.

Example 2
Production rises from 40 units to 50 units. The percentage increase is

  1. 10%
  2. 20%
  3. 25%
  4. 50%

Solution

  1. Increase = 10 on a base of 40.
  2. (10/40) × 100 = 25%.

Answer: C. The base is the earlier year, not the later one.

Example 3
A family’s monthly expenditure is Rs 36000, and food takes a 90° sector in the pie chart. The expenditure on food is

  1. Rs 6000
  2. Rs 9000
  3. Rs 12000
  4. Rs 18000

Solution

  1. 90/360 = 1/4.
  2. Food = 36000/4 = 9000.

Answer: B. A right angle on a pie chart is one quarter of the money.

Example 4
The standard deviation of the list 1, 1, 5, 5, using division by n, is

  1. 1
  2. 2
  3. 4
  4. √2

Solution

  1. Mean = 12/4 = 3.
  2. Squared deviations: 4, 4, 4 and 4. Sum = 16.
  3. Variance = 16/4 = 4. Standard deviation = 2.

Answer: B. The two values sit 2 units on either side of the mean, and the variance comes out a perfect square.

Example 5
For a distribution with mean 20 and median 18, the empirical estimate of the mode is

  1. 14
  2. 16
  3. 18
  4. 22

Solution

  1. Mode ≈ 3 × median − 2 × mean.
  2. 3 × 18 − 2 × 20 = 54 − 40 = 14.

Answer: A. Call it an estimate. A small raw list can have a different actual mode.
