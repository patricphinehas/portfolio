Moments, skewness and kurtosis

Moments summarise a distribution the way a mean and a variance do, and then one step further. Skewness says whether one tail is longer. Kurtosis says whether the peak and the tails are heavier than a normal curve.

You need the meanings and the standard coefficients, not a long proof.

What SSC lists

  • Raw and central moments, and the relation between them at the level of the definitions.
  • Skewness and its common measures.
  • Kurtosis and the normal-curve benchmark.
  • The difference between a measure of spread and a measure of shape.

Rules and formulas

Moments

The first raw moment about zero is the mean.
Central moments are taken about the mean. The second central moment is the variance.
The third central moment is used for skewness. The fourth is used for kurtosis.

Skewness

A longer right tail is positive skew. A longer left tail is negative skew. A symmetric distribution has skewness zero.
Karl Pearson’s coefficient = (mean − mode) / standard deviation.
The moment coefficient is the third central moment divided by the cube of the standard deviation.
Bowley’s measure = (Q3 + Q1 − 2Q2) / (Q3 − Q1).

Kurtosis

The moment coefficient β2 = μ4 / μ2².
For a normal distribution β2 = 3. That case is mesokurtic.
β2 greater than 3 is leptokurtic, a sharper peak and heavier tails in the usual description. β2 less than 3 is platykurtic.

What they are not

Skewness is not dispersion. Two distributions can have the same standard deviation and different skewness.

Quick methods

From raw moments about any point: μ₂ = μ₂′ − μ₁′², μ₃ = μ₃′ − 3μ₂′μ₁′ + 2μ₁′³.
β₁ = μ₃²/μ₂³. γ₁ = √β₁ with the sign of μ₃. Excess kurtosis γ₂ = β₂ − 3.
Pearson’s second coefficient (using the median) = 3(mean − median)/SD.
Bowley’s coefficient lies between −1 and +1.

Common traps

Pearson’s skewness uses mean minus mode, not mode minus mean, if you want the sign to match a right tail.

Bowley’s formula has Q3 + Q1 − 2 × median in the numerator, because Q2 is the median.

β2 for the normal curve is 3, not 0 and not 1.

Leptokurtic is the more peaked case in this terminology, the one with β2 above 3.

Worked examples

Read the solution only after you have tried the question. Options are in exam style.

Example 1
A distribution with a longer tail on the right has

  1. negative skew
  2. positive skew
  3. zero variance
  4. no mean

Solution

  1. The long tail is the direction of the skew.
  2. A right tail is positive skew.

Answer: B. The bulk of the data can still sit on the left.

Example 2
Karl Pearson’s skewness is

  1. (mean − mode) / standard deviation
  2. (mode − mean) / variance
  3. Q3 − Q1
  4. β2

Solution

  1. The coefficient uses the gap between mean and mode.
  2. It is scaled by the standard deviation.

Answer: A. Q3 − Q1 is a dispersion gap. β2 is kurtosis.

Example 3
For a normal distribution, β2 equals

  1. 0
  2. 1
  3. 3
  4. n

Solution

  1. β2 = μ4 / μ2².
  2. The normal benchmark is 3.

Answer: C. That benchmark is what mesokurtic refers to.

Example 4
Bowley’s coefficient uses

  1. mean and mode
  2. quartiles
  3. only the range
  4. only β2

Solution

  1. The formula is (Q3 + Q1 − 2Q2) / (Q3 − Q1).
  2. It needs no mean.

Answer: B. It is useful when the distribution is given by quartiles, or when the mean is a poor summary.
